School of Traditional Skills · Messaging Analysis

What the 2025 Summit Ads Said

Every headline, primary text, on-image overlay and in-video line from the fall 2025 Traditional Skills Summit campaigns. Everything here is ranked against the 2025 flight itself, not against 2023 and 2024, so "did well" means did well in the market STS was actually buying in.

Start here: the two real problems

There are exactly two things that went wrong in 2025, and neither of them is the ad copy. Traffic cost nearly twice as much to buy as in 2024, and the traffic that did arrive converted at two thirds the rate. Everything else in this report sits underneath those two facts.

The single most important number in this report is not an ad number.

The ads got better at earning attention in 2025. CTR of 3.36% is the highest of the three years, beating 2024's 3.09% and 2023's 2.69%. What collapsed is what happened after the click: only 24% of link clicks became registrations, against 36% in 2023 and 35% in 2024. If the 2025 page had converted at the 2024 rate, the same 48,646 link clicks would have produced roughly 17,026 registrations at about $3.59 each instead of $5.14. The registration page, not the ad copy, is where most of 2025 went wrong.

And it was not the tracking link.

85% of 2025 spend ($51,901) pointed at a redirect domain, www.ststrk1.com/53DD73/3TJ7N7/, instead of the direct schooloftraditionalskills.com/summit used in 2023 and 2024. That is worth knowing, but it is not the culprit: click-to-registration was 24.4% through the redirect and 25.4% direct. Both are far below the 35% of prior years, so the problem sits on the page or in the offer, not in the hop.

The message changed completely in 2025

2023 and 2024 ran a 23 to 29 word plain offer. 2025 replaced it with a 173-word food-security argument, and put 78% of the entire budget behind it.

What this ad does that 2023 and 2024 never did.
  • Opens on a question with a real consequence. "If the grocery store shelves were empty tomorrow, could you still feed your family next week?" Prior years opened on the offer.
  • Names five instructors and a count. Joel Salatin, Shawn and Beth Dougherty, Justin Rhodes, Melissa K Norris, Carolyn Thomas, plus "25 homesteading experts and authors". 2024 named nobody. 2023 named one person.
  • Puts the logistics on their own line. 📅 FREE | Online | September 22-26 reads instantly, even in a truncated feed preview.
  • Uses outcome bullets, each starting with a verb in caps. GROW, PRESERVE, RAISE, COOK, PREPARE. Every bullet pairs the action with the emotional payoff ("so you never worry about shortages again", "for peace of mind year-round").
  • Closes on removing friction, not on urgency. "Attend from anywhere. It's FREE to join!"

The finding that reverses the 2023 and 2024 rule

In 2023 and 2024, short copy beat long copy every time and the penalty for long copy was roughly three times the cost per registration. In 2025 the opposite happened, on both image and video.

Read this carefully before you act on it.

The long-copy ads got 78% of the budget and the short-copy ads got a few hundred dollars each, so this is a comparison between a funded message and unfunded ones. That is a serious confound and it means this is not proof that long copy is now better. What it does prove is narrower and still useful: a 173-word argument-led ad can carry a whole flight without fatiguing, at a frequency of only 2.52, which the 2023 and 2024 data would not have predicted. The right read is that the plain 23-word offer and the long food-security argument are two different tools, and 2025 showed the long one can be the primary rather than a closing-days experiment. It also means the short variants were never actually tested.

The exact 2024 control ran again in 2025 with a date and an emoji added. It received $71 of asset-level spend across two variants, against $47,687 for the long ad. There is not enough delivery there to call it beaten. It was benched, not tested.

Why there is no verdict in this table.

Two headlines took $47,518 of the $48,723 that Meta reported at headline level. The remaining five split $1,206 between them, which is nowhere near enough delivery to separate a headline from the ad set and creative it happened to land in. Anything that looks like a pattern here, an emoji, a word, a running order, is a hypothesis for the next flight, not a finding from this one.

The practical implication is about how the slot is run, not about which line to pick. Headlines are the cheapest thing in the account to vary and the fastest to read, so they should be in a deliberate, evenly-weighted test in every flight, with the variants held to a small number and given comparable delivery. 2025 did the opposite: seven variants, wildly uneven spend, no structure. That is why there is nothing to learn from it.

Three things to fix in the slot itself.
  • The event has four different names across the headlines. "Homesteading summit", "Homesteading Summit", "Homesteading Conference" and "Virtual Homesteading Event" all ran in the same flight. This is a brand consistency problem regardless of what any of them cost, and it also makes headline testing impossible to read, because a naming change and a wording change are moving at the same time.
  • Lowercase "summit" in the two highest-spending headlines. Small thing, but it is the brand's own event name.
  • The link description slot was left completely empty. All $48,635 of asset-level 2025 spend ran with no description. In 2023 and 2024 the same line ran in both years: "The only virtual conference giving you the homesteading skills you need for an authentic life you love." That is a free slot under the headline that 2025 gave up.

Why the click cost nearly double

Cost per link click went from $0.67 in 2024 to $1.26 in 2025, an increase of 87%. That is the single biggest driver of the year's cost per registration, and it is a media-buying outcome, not a copy outcome.

It was not saturation. Frequency went down, not up.

Average frequency fell to 2.52 in 2025 from 3.41 in 2024 and 3.81 in 2023. At ad set level the top spenders ran between 1.24 and 1.65. Nobody was being shown these ads too often, and no creative was worn out. The auction simply charged more per person reached: 28,510 people reached per $1,000 in 2025 against 36,306 in 2024 and 57,060 in 2023.

The account bought from a much narrower pool

Some auction inflation is market-wide and outside anyone's control. But 2025 also made a series of choices that each removed cheap inventory from the buy, and they compound.

Thirty creative files is not diversification. It is one message in thirty wrappers.

The file count looks varied and the message was not. 98% of all copy-level spend ran a single 173-word body, against roughly 50% in each of the two prior years. Eighty-seven percent of headline spend ran one headline. On the buying side the number of placements taking real budget fell from 5 to 3 and the buy concentrated to 94% United States. Some of that narrowing was the right call on the numbers, Instagram in particular. The problem is that nothing replaced it. By the end the whole flight was one argument, running in two Facebook placements, in one country. That is not enough surface area for $61,106, and the $1.26 click is the invoice for it.

The ad set count moved the wrong way.

2025 ran 159 ad sets, 112 of them narrow single-interest sets like "Crop (agriculture)", "Emergency management", "Camping" and "Fishing". More ad sets is not more diversification, it is the same audience sliced thinner. Each slice carries its own learning requirement and its own minimum viable budget, and on $61,106 most of them never got either. Diversify the message and the inventory, consolidate the ad sets.

The Josh video family bought the cheapest attention in the account by a wide margin.

The jv videos posted click costs of $0.51 and $0.52, roughly 60% below the 2025 average of $1.26, on CTRs of 8.61% and 8.97%, which are more than 2.5 times the year's average. This is where the cheap traffic was and almost none of the budget went there.

Carousels were the year's best untested idea.

Carousels were new for 2025 and took $3,670 across five variants. They are not efficient on registrations yet, but Carov1-cs bought clicks at $0.72 (42% under average) and _Carousel1 posted a 4.02% CTR (20% above average) with a 30.2% hook rate. On this evidence the format earns a proper test at real budget, not another $165.

Broad beat narrow, and warm audiences held up.

The single best-performing ad set of the year was "Homesteading (home & garden)" at $3.91, 24% under the average, on $5,453. It is also the broadest interest in the account. "Organic farming" followed at $3.98. Both beat the largest ad set, "Do it yourself (DIY)" at $9,636 and $4.77, and both crushed "Sustainable agriculture", which took $7,587 at $5.73, 11% worse than average and the most expensive large ad set in the flight.

Three of the fourteen winners were lookalikes: STS Full Marketing list at $4.14, STS Members list at $4.20, and 2025 Summit Signups at $4.34. Warm and lookalike audiences were reliably cheaper than the narrow interest slices, and they collectively received 11% of budget.

The two worst buys were both narrow and both avoidable.

"HSF FB Engagers 365" at $8.52 (66% worse than average) and "homeschooled" at $8.36 (63% worse) took $1,668 between them. "Crop (agriculture)", split across two ad sets, took $3,758 at $5.14 and $7.13. Those are the slices to cut first.

What to carry into this year

Keep, and fund properly
  • iihh-1-CL and the "Take Back Control of Your Food & Health" overlay. The only creative that beat the year's average cost per registration at scale. Make it the control.
  • The 173-word food-security body. $4.77, 7% under the year's average, and it carried the flight without fatiguing at a frequency of 2.52.
  • The jv video family. Cheapest clicks in the account at $0.51 to $0.52, CTR over 8.5%, and the cheapest registration of the year at $4.23. It received $216.
  • Broad interests over narrow slices. "Homesteading (home & garden)" at $3.91 beat every narrow agricultural interest in the account.
  • Lookalikes off the STS lists. Three of the fourteen winning ad sets, all between $4.14 and $4.34.
  • Named experts in the copy and the verb-led outcome bullets.
  • The headline slot as a standing test. Nothing in 2025 earned a headline verdict, so treat every line as unproven and run two or three evenly-funded variants from the first day of the flight.
  • Carousels. Cheap clicks and strong CTR on $3,670. Give the format a real test.
Change
  • Find new inventory before you touch the copy. Placements taking real budget fell from 5 to 3 and the buy is now 94% United States. Instagram was rightly cut on cost, so the job is finding and testing replacements, not reinstating it. That is the most direct lever on the $1.26 click.
  • Write more than one message. 98% of copy spend ran one body. Three or four genuinely different arguments, each funded, is the diversification that matters. Thirty files of the same message is not.
  • Consolidate the ad sets. 159 ad sets on $61,106, 112 of them single-interest slices. Fewer, broader ad sets with real budget.
  • Cut the losers early. HSF FB Engagers ($8.52), homeschooled ($8.36) and Crop (agriculture) took $5,426 between them at well above average cost.
  • Fix the registration page. 24% of link clicks converted against 35% in 2024. Worth more than every other item on this list.
  • One event name, everywhere. Four names ran across the 2025 headlines. Pick one and use it in every headline, every image and on the page, so headline tests measure wording rather than naming.
  • Test headlines properly. Two or three variants, evenly funded, changing one element at a time. Seven variants with five of them splitting $1,206 teaches nothing.
  • Refill the empty link description slot and capitalise Summit.
  • Reinstate the 23-word plain offer as a funded control, not a $71 afterthought.
